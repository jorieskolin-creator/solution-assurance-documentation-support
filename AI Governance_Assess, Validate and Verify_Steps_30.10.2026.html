AI governance and solution architecture

A general assessment framework

Assess the solution, not only the model.

A practical way to decide what an AI solution may conclude or do, what data it may use, which architecture is proportionate, and what evidence is required before release.

Purpose and consequence

Define how the output will be used and what can happen when it is wrong.

Evidence and distance

Understand how far the conclusion sits from directly verifiable evidence.

Data and permission

Establish what data may be processed, by which service, under which obligations.

Model approval is necessary but not sufficient. An approved enterprise service can reduce certain supplier and data handling risks. It does not establish that the purpose is permitted, the source data is sound, access rights are correct, the answer is justified, or the resulting action is acceptable.

The same model can support a low risk drafting tool or a consequential decision process. The difference lies in the complete solution, its data flows, its authority, its controls, and its ownership.

The model is one part of the information processing chain. Trust belongs to the whole solution.

Trust follows architecture, not capability alone

Requirement levels A, B, and C describe where authority sits. They are alternative design responses, not maturity stages.

Conceptual illustration. Better grounding can improve an answer without transferring authority away from the model. A governed architecture defines what evidence is sufficient and what may become an accepted conclusion. Actual trust still requires testing and operational evidence.

The central shift in governance

A model centred approach asks whether the model is acceptable. A solution centred approach also asks whether the complete use of that model is acceptable.

Regulatory classification and model approval are separate inputs. Neither establishes the complete solution risk or release acceptance.

Frame Purpose and Permission

Steps 1 to 3 establish whether the proposed use makes sense, what is at stake, and whether the processing is allowed. Model selection comes after these decisions.

Step 1

Define intended use and the role of the output

Start with the work and its consequence, not with technology.

  • What problem is being solved, and what value is expected?
  • Who uses the output, and who can be affected by it?
  • Is the output a draft, a summary, a recommendation, a decision, or an action?
  • Which material outputs, recommendations, decisions, and actions are possible, including downstream and automated use?
  • Who makes the final decision and owns its consequence?
  • Which uses are explicitly outside scope?
Output: An intended use record covering users, affected parties, output, decision role, permitted autonomy, and prohibited uses.
Step 2

Assess impact and assign the solution risk level

The important question is not only how often the model may err. It is what happens when it does.

  • What is the worst credible error, misuse, or disclosure?
  • Can the effect harm a person, a customer, or the organisation?
  • Can the error be detected before it has an effect?
  • Can the action be reversed and the harm genuinely repaired?
  • How many cases can be processed before the first error is found?
  • Can the output spread to other systems or external recipients?
Output: A documented risk level with reasons. A severe single consequence can determine the level even when other factors appear low.
Step 3

Confirm permission and establish data rules

A low impact error does not make otherwise prohibited processing acceptable.

  • Is the intended processing permitted for this specific purpose?
  • Do privacy duties, contracts, confidentiality, or customer commitments restrict it?
  • What personal, confidential, or otherwise sensitive data is involved?
  • Can calculations, aggregates, summaries, embeddings, model inputs, and other derived data remain sensitive or reveal information through inference?
  • Can the purpose be achieved with less data or less sensitive data?
  • Are source quality, provenance, currency, and usage rights sufficient?
  • Are any further legal, privacy, security, or domain assessments required?
Decision: Proceed, proceed with restrictions, redesign, or stop. Record source and derived data classes and the conditions for their use.

Lower consequence

Errors are limited, visible, and cheap to reverse.

Typical response

Baseline controls and proportionate sampling may support delegation.

Elevated consequence

Errors have meaningful effects, are harder to detect, or can spread widely.

Typical response

Bounded autonomy, stronger validation, explicit gates, and targeted human review.

High consequence

Effects are severe, difficult to reverse, or affect important decisions.

Typical response

Human decision authority, strong assurance, strict action limits, or a decision not to proceed.

Design the System and Its Authority

Steps 4 and 5 translate purpose, risk, evidence needs, and data obligations into architecture. The central choice is how much authority the model should hold.

Step 4

Map data, services, decisions, and responsibilities

Describe the complete information chain rather than only the model endpoint.

  • Where does data originate, and how is its quality maintained?
  • What is removed or transformed before the model call?
  • Which service, configuration, contract, and processing location are used?
  • What is retained across inputs, prompts, outputs, provider and platform processing, application storage, logs, telemetry, temporary files, caches, backups, retrieval indexes, vector stores, and exports?
  • Are retrieval and document access filtered by each user’s permissions?
  • Who can see the output, and can it trigger a later action?
  • Where are the trust boundaries and likely security threats?

A contract alone is not operating evidence. Contractual commitments, actual service configuration, and provider side processing must agree.

Output: A data flow and architecture record with named owners for the provider, shared platform, solution, and business process.
Step 5

Select requirement level A, B, or C

The levels describe different implementation requirements. Choose the least complex level that satisfies the actual need. Do not build C when A is sufficient, and do not use A when the conclusion requires governed evidence.

A

Generate

The model produces a draft or transformation. Its fluent answer is not treated as evidence of correctness.

Appropriate when

The task is ideation, language work, drafting, or another use where errors are limited and easy to repair.

Authority

The model generates. The user or process owns how the output is used.

Core controls

Approved service, data minimisation, clear scope, user guidance, and no unapproved consequential action.

Use when free generation meets the requirement
B

Ground

Retrieval, tools, sources, and checks improve the material available to the model.

Appropriate when

The answer should remain close to identifiable sources, such as extraction, summarisation, or source based questions.

Authority

The model may still decide what matters, whether enough evidence exists, and when the answer is complete.

Core controls

Source permissions, provenance, freshness, retrieval testing, useful citations, and careful treatment of untrusted content.

Use when better evidence support is sufficient
C

Govern

The solution defines what evidence is required and what may become an accepted conclusion.

Appropriate when

The task involves consequential analysis, explicit criteria, multiple sources, material gaps, or significant inference.

Authority

The model performs bounded work. External rules and states control validation, publication, and action.

Core controls

Evidence and inference rules, provenance, contradiction handling, deterministic calculations, controlled states, UNKNOWN and BLOCK outcomes, and human decision authority where required.

Use when conclusions must be entitled and defensible
Data governance applies at every level. Level C does not make prohibited data acceptable. Level A does not imply that data is public. Service choice, retention, access rights, minimisation, and lifecycle controls remain separate decisions.

The levels require different flows of authority

These simplified architectures show what changes between A, B, and C. They are design patterns rather than mandatory components. The key difference is not the amount of technology. It is how evidence enters the work and who or what may accept the result.

A · Generate

A direct path from request to draft

  1. The user supplies a task and permitted data
  2. The model generates a candidate output
  3. The user or business process reviews and decides how to use it

Architectural implicationResponsibility for acceptance remains outside the model, but the pattern itself adds no independent evidence or release control.

B · Ground

A source supported path with retrieval

  1. The user supplies a task and permitted data
  2. The solution retrieves sources under the user’s permissions
  3. The model generates an answer using the retrieved material
  4. References and source material support review

Architectural implicationRetrieval improves the available evidence. The model can still influence source selection, interpretation, completeness, and the decision to answer.

C · Govern

A controlled path from task to accepted result

  1. The cognitive model defines the task, required evidence, criteria, and permitted conclusions
  2. The solution retrieves permitted sources with provenance and state
  3. The model performs bounded analysis or transformation
  4. Separate controls check evidence, rules, calculations, and action limits
  5. The solution releases an entitled result or returns UNKNOWN or BLOCK

Architectural implicationAcceptance is an external system decision. Human authority remains where judgement or accountability cannot be delegated.

Data governance alone cannot create this distinction. Sound permissions, quality, retention, and provenance are essential at every level. Moving from information support to governed acceptance also requires changes in flow, control points, state, and authority.

Epistemic distance changes the design problem

The further a conclusion sits from directly verifiable evidence, the more important explicit criteria, inference limits, and refusal become.

Conceptual illustration, not a performance promise. Grounding can be highly effective for source bound tasks. Govern becomes valuable when the answer is not simply present in the source material but must be derived under explicit rules.

The cognitive model belongs to the solution

Here, a cognitive model means the solution’s explicit representation of the domain, evidence, permitted inferences, decision criteria, states, and publication rules. It is not the language model itself.

A prompt can request a behaviour. Only the surrounding system can enforce it. Agents and tools may still perform useful work, but they should execute a defined method rather than invent the method as they go.

Deterministic gates are valuable where conditions can be formalised. Bounded semantic review may be needed where interpretation remains. Human judgement is required where accountability or normative choice cannot be delegated. Determinism makes a rule enforceable, not correct.

Make the cognitive model operational through knowledge contracts

A cognitive model becomes operational when its domain concepts and rules are translated into explicit instructions for knowledge use. A context contract defines what the solution may retrieve and place before the model. A verification contract defines what must be checked before the result can be accepted, published, or used for action.

A zero trust approach treats prompts, retrieved content, tool results, and model output as unverified until the relevant contract has been satisfied. The model may help interpret a request or propose a query. The surrounding solution validates that query against the context contract before execution. The model does not grant itself access, reduce the evidence requirement, or approve its own result.

These contracts should be identity bound, versioned, validated, and enforced by the surrounding solution. The databases remain managed knowledge sources. The cognitive model defines the method for searching, retrieving, combining, and using their content.

Generation and acceptance are separate acts. Reliability can sometimes mean UNKNOWN or BLOCK.

Prove and Sustain Trust

Steps 6 and 7 turn architecture into demonstrated assurance. Trust is not a property claimed once. It is continuously tested against defined expectations.

Step 6

Test, validate, and approve with evidence

Production approval requires more than a successful demonstration or an approved model.

  • Are quality measures and acceptance thresholds linked to the actual consequence?
  • Do test cases cover normal work, boundaries, missing evidence, contradictions, and misuse?
  • Are field definitions, filtering rules, and calculations validated against independently prepared expected results?
  • Are cases used for tuning kept separate from cases used for validation?
  • Have access control, tenant separation, security, and data lifecycle controls been tested?
  • Can the system reject, abstain, stop, roll back, and return to a trusted state?
  • Is human review competent, informed, empowered, and placed before consequential action?
  • Who accepts residual risk and approves release?
Output: Test evidence, exceptions, control owners, release approval, operational limits, and reassessment conditions.
Step 7

Operate, monitor, and reassess

The purpose of monitoring is to detect when the solution no longer deserves the trust assigned to it.

  • Are output quality, false acceptance, false blocking, abstention, and overrides monitored?
  • Are data quality, access anomalies, security events, cost, and business value visible?
  • Who responds to alerts, incidents, and unexpected behaviour?
  • Can processing be stopped and work transferred to a safe alternative?
  • Are rules, sources, models, prompts, tools, and permissions under change control?

Reassess when the intended use, affected population, autonomy, data sources, permissions, model, retrieval, tools, or publication rules change. A change in use can alter risk even when the model remains the same.

Output: Named operational ownership, monitoring evidence, incident procedures, and a continuing reassessment cycle.

Cognition

What should the solution conclude, and under which domain rules?

Control

What may the solution accept, publish, or do, and when must it stop?

Assurance

How do we demonstrate that cognition and control remain correct?

Responsibility across the solution

Shared services create consistency. They do not remove solution ownership. Each control needs an accountable person and evidence that it operates as intended.

The minimum assessment record

  1. Intended use, users, affected parties, owner, and prohibited uses
  2. Consequence analysis and solution risk level
  3. Permission, source and derived data classes, service basis, and retention rules
  4. Data flow, responsibilities, and selected level A, B, or C
  5. Evidence, authority, human oversight, and action controls
  6. Test results, release approval, exceptions, and residual risk
  7. Monitoring, incident response, and reassessment triggers

Start with what the output will do and what happens when it is wrong. Then give the model only the authority that the evidence, consequence, and controls justify.
